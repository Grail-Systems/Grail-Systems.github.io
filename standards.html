The Deep East Texas Standard

Texas counties cannot zone or ban data centers. But they sign the road agreements, tax abatements and payments in lieu of taxes these projects need, and a contract can protect what an ordinance cannot. These are the terms every county should ask for. Copy them freely.

Written by Fawn Elizabeth Zion and the East Texas Legacy Coalition for Angelina County, October 2026. Published by Grail Systems.

Why this is not extreme

In July 2026, Governor Abbott called for data centers to use closed-loop cooling, report their water and electricity use every year, connect to the grid in ways that lower household bills, and give up unnecessary incentives. A Henderson County developer withdrew rather than meet those standards. Many of the terms below already appear in Texas county drafts and in agreements in Virginia and Pennsylvania. In June 2026, the City of Fort Worth proposed its own data center rules with 250-foot setbacks from homes, generators kept 300 feet away, a crypto mining ban, closed-loop cooling and stricter noise limits.

Moratoriums are risky. Hill County passed one in May 2026, was sued for $100 million, and rescinded it within weeks. Contract terms and written abatement guidelines hold up better.

Start with these three

  • 1Public hearings before any agreement is signed

    With every draft posted at least two weeks before the vote.

  • 2A hard daily water cap in every agreement

    Written into the contract, with metered public reporting.

  • 3Put it all in the county's tax abatement guidelines

    State law requires counties to adopt guidelines before granting any abatement and review them every two years. Standards written there apply to every applicant from the start.

16 conditions for every agreement

For every road-use agreement, road closure, tax abatement or payment in lieu of taxes for a data center, crypto mine or the power plant that serves it.

  • 1Public process

    Public hearings before approval, drafts posted two weeks ahead, no secret terms.

  • 2Who stands behind it

    The parent company guarantees every obligation, binding on any future owner, with proof of financing.

  • 3Ownership, use and size

    Disclose the real owner, certify no foreign adversary control under the Lone Star Infrastructure Protection Act, bar crypto mining or any change in use without a new hearing, and cap the size in megawatts and acres.

  • 4One project, one set of rules

    Disclose every affiliate, contractor and permit across every agency, so a project cannot be split into small pieces that each escape scrutiny.

  • 5Truly independent experts

    The county picks the experts and the developer pays through the county. No expert who worked for the developer in five years. Reports and raw data made public.

  • 6Water and sewer

    An independent water study, a hard daily cap, closed-loop cooling with no evaporative towers, public metered reporting, waterway setbacks and testing, conservation that saves at least as much water as the facility uses, and full payment for any sewer upgrades or damage.

  • 7Nearby homes and wells

    Baseline testing of private wells, ongoing monitoring, repair or replacement of any well that declines, and a buyout guarantee for homes made unlivable.

  • 8Noise and vibration

    A limit based on measured background levels, never above 55 decibels at any home, farm or ranch, covering low-frequency hum and generators, with permanent monitoring.

  • 9Power and air

    Backup generation disclosed to the county, best available emission controls, and the developer pays every grid upgrade so none of it lands on household bills.

  • 10Land, light and setbacks

    Limit forest clearing, protect creeks and wetlands, Dark Sky lighting, and real setbacks and tree buffers next to homes.

  • 11Roads, construction and housing

    County-set repairs and truck routes, no road closures without a hearing and an equal route, limits on years-long construction noise, and a worker housing plan.

  • 12Emergency services and backup power

    Funding for fire and ambulance service, a hazardous materials inventory and response plan, and backup generators for rural water systems and shelters.

  • 13Jobs, schools and community benefit

    Real job and wage numbers, training with the local college, first consideration for truly local businesses, school support, clawbacks, published incentive values, and a secured community fund with residents deciding how it is spent.

  • 14Equipment and waste

    Certified recycling of servers and batteries, secure data destruction, and no electronic waste left in the county.

  • 15Financial protection

    A guaranteed minimum annual payment that cannot shrink on appraisal protest, bonding for roads and decommissioning, insurance, and indemnification.

  • 16Enforcement and future law

    Annual compliance reports, inspections, a 24-hour complaint line, escalating penalties, the right to go to court, and a reopener if the Legislature gives counties new authority.

8 things a county can do beyond any single deal

  • AWrite the standards into abatement guidelines

    With an application fee, and the developer paying the county's legal and engineering review.

  • BStudy every project together

    The combined water, power, road and noise impact of all proposals at once.

  • CBring every agency to one table

    County, city, groundwater district and neighboring counties, so no developer can play one against another.

  • DGive residents a seat

    A citizen advisory committee that reviews applications before votes.

  • ETake specific bills to Austin

    County noise authority outside city limits, disclosure of backup generation and water use, data centers added to the Lone Star Infrastructure Protection Act, a county voice in large out-of-city water sales, and protection for household electric bills.

  • FMeasure what is already here

    A professional baseline sound study near existing operations, as Hood County did.

  • GReclaim old industrial sites on the community's terms

    EPA Brownfields grants and TCEQ cleanup programs, and ask residents what the land should become.

  • HCount the real jobs

    Permanent local jobs in writing, weighed against what the land, water and incentives could do in other hands.
