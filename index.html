Ground Truth Texas

Companies are planning data centers, crypto mines and power plants across Texas, often before neighbors hear a word.

This is about protecting what we all share: our water, our land, our neighbors and the communities we’re raising our families in. Ground Truth Texas lifts the veil. We dig these plans out of the public record and put them where everyone can see them, so the people who live here decide what happens here. And we don’t leave you standing there. We give you the tools, the records and the neighbors to do something about it.

They heard you. Then they passed it anyway.

On Monday, Nov. 9, people from Angelina and Nacogdoches counties spent hours speaking against new groundwater rules at a Pineywoods Groundwater Conservation District hearing, and the district received a flood of written comments for the record. Then the board passed the rules exactly as written. The full story

This isn’t just one board on one night. It’s the pattern across Texas. Decisions about our water, land and power get made in rooms most people never hear about, on timelines built so that by the time neighbors find out, the vote is already done.

The same story, county after county

A “plastics plant” that wasn’t.

In Liberty County, a company told officials it was building a plant to make plastics for PPE. The county learned from a Houston newspaper that it was a data center, now being built with a 240 MW gas plant. See it on the map

“Zero water,” says the ad.

Near Marfa, a project advertises zero water for cooling. A county official reported it plans to pump about 714,000 gallons of groundwater a day, more than the whole City of Marfa uses. See it on the map

$150 million, and no biorefinery.

At the old Bon Wier mill site in Newton County, $150 million in incentives never produced the promised biorefinery. Now the site is being marketed to data centers. Newton County

The first Texas city to ban data centers. Hays County

Hood County won in court.

Developers sued, claiming two data center campuses should be approved automatically because the county missed a 30-day deadline. A judge dismissed the suit. Hood County

What it means for you

When one of these projects comes to town, it raises questions about your water, the power grid, noise, and what happens to the taxes and land around you. Most of them need water and power on a huge scale, and here in Deep East Texas, everyone drinks from the same Carrizo-Wilcox Aquifer.
